**Author of the entry:** Mariano Saggiomo

With the exception of the attribution to Francesco Queirolo (1704–1762) proposed in the 1772 edition of Pompeo Sarnelli’s guide (p. 133), the sources agree that the _Decoro_ is the work of Antonio Corradini (see, at least, Origlia Paolino 1754, p. 365; Sigismondo 1788, pp. 37–38; Galante 1872, p. 161), despite the fact that the date 1755 incised in the inscription is three years later than the death of the Venetian artist. The autograph status, or at any rate the conception of the subject, is in fact confirmed by the same compositional scheme adopted by the sculptor in the _Pudicizia_ here in the Chapel and earlier still in the _Vestale_ of Palazzo Barberini, executed in Rome in 1743 (cf. Riccoboni 1952, pp. 155–156; Cogo 1996, pp. 296–301, no. 40; for comparisons with Austrian works the artist may have seen, cf. Cioffi 1994, p. 29). However, Marina Picone (1959, p. 72) observed in the _Decoro_ ‘a want of equilibrium, a stylistic decline […], as if it were a work executed on that model by an imitator who has reproduced the external features of the composition with absolute fidelity without truly entering into its spirit and sensibility’ – doubts which Picone extends, this time in the opposite sense, to the portraits of Isabella Tolfa and Laudomia Milano, set in the _tondo_ at the top of the statue, suggesting that they may perhaps be by Queirolo, since they ‘stand out on account of their loftier register’ compared with others in the church (ibid., p. 68).

Dedicated, therefore, to the first and second wives of Giovan Francesco di Sangro, third Prince of Sansevero, the monument is inspired by the model proposed in Cesare Ripa’s _Iconologia_, the late sixteenth-century emblematic repertory in which, as has been widely demonstrated (cf. Mâle 1927; Panfili 2015; and, most recently, Forgione 2022, pp. 47–49), Raimondo consistently showed great interest, even financing its substantial eighteenth-century edition in five volumes, edited by Cesare Orlandi between 1764 and 1767. Raimondo drew heavily on this catalogue to establish the iconography of the works in his family chapel. In the present case, however, this did not suffice to satisfy the Prince’s expectations; he in fact listed the _Decoro_ among the works deemed ‘expendable’, that is, replaceable, should a more capable sculptor be found to adapt them to his own artistic taste (_Testamento_ 1770, fols. 46r–v; cf. also _Chartulae desangriane_ 2006, p. 107).

Corresponding to Ripa’s description (1765, pp. 125–126), the _Decoro_ is a ‘youth of fair and honourable appearance’ wearing the _leonté_, symbol of the strength of virtue. The lifeless head of the animal rests on a short, fluted column (absent in Ripa), which simultaneously serves as a base for the cartouche bearing the motto _sic floret decoro decus_, indicating that beauty is a reflection of decorum. The two different items of footwear worn by the figure, the aristocratic _coturno_ on the right foot and the humble _soccus_ on the left, similarly follow Ripa’s model, signifying that ‘each person, in matters of dress, must have regard for the observance of decorum according to his age and station’ (ibid., p. 136).

Limiting ourselves to the principal divergence between sculpture and engraving, and, bearing in mind that, as is customary with the Di Sangro family chapel, the work need not be read as a precise transposition of the literary model but merely as inspired by it, it should be observed that the youth in the illustration is not semi-nude as in the statue, but clothed in a tunic with floral motifs, an iconographically significant detail, since it is decorated with leaves of the amaranth, ‘a flower which blooms at all times and preserves the decorum of its beauty’ (ibid., p. 128). The twig of the same plant shown in the figure’s left hand may correspond to the ‘branch of fruit’ which, according to the inventory of the remaining goods in Raimondo di Sangro’s estate drawn up in 1771, the statue bore in its left hand (_Inventario_ 1771, fol. 106v; cf. also Attanasio 2011, p. 145; Colapietra 1986, p. 74), but is now lost. The same document further records that the inscription was ‘to the right of the said statue’, that is, according to the well-founded interpretation of Oderisio de Sangro (1991, p. 214), on the same flat slab still appearing to the right of the figure in a lithograph by Franz Wenzel (probably dated 1839), examined by de Sangro but currently untraceable. The slab, formerly in the sacristy (ibid.), is now in the Chapel’s storerooms.

Following damage to the inscription during the nineteenth-century collapse, it was recopied onto the pedestal of the statue, filling the gap left by the bas-relief of _Susanna and the Elders_, mentioned by Origlia (1754, p. 365). Picone (1959, p. 72) suggests that the relief was removed by Raimondo himself in order to deflect accusations ‘directed against the curious decorative character of the chapel’, whereas, for Colapietra (1986, pp. 74–75), the piece was originally joined to the _Pudicizia_, which, in a reconstruction bordering on the fanciful, would have formed a single group with the _Mestizia_ and the _Decoro_, the latter defined by the scholar as ‘no more than an accompanying statue’. Given that several works underwent alterations even before Wenzel’s survey (see, for example, entries 15 and 17 on the holy water stoups), it is legitimate to ask whether the inscription of the _Decoro_ may originally have been located in the empty cartouche above the monument to the third Prince of Sansevero in an arrangement that would have physically joined the monuments of the relatives as well.

In conclusion, in 1838, Stanislao D’Aloe (p. 24) recorded the presence of the statue of the _Decoro_ in the sacristy, ‘having been removed from the chapel because the southern wall was threatening collapse’. The structural problems of the wall that eventually collapsed at the end of the century (see, especially, Colonna di Stigliano 1895), and which Chiarini described in 1858 as ‘closed off with masonry’ (p. 444), must therefore date from considerably earlier.
