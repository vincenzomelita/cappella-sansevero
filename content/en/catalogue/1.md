**Author of the entry:** Gianluca Forgione

As recorded in the reprint of the Breve nota di quel che si vede in casa del principe di Sansevero don Raimondo di Sangro nella città di Napoli (1769, p. 9; cf. Attanasio 2011, pp. 58–59), the Compianto represents ‘the most distinguished work’ of Francesco Celebrano, alone enough ‘to immortalise his name’ (Napoli Signorelli 1811, p. 260). Traditionally identified as a ‘Descent from the Cross’, the relief in fact depicts the moment after Christ has been taken _down_ from the cross and John and the holy women mourn over the laid-out corpse. This clarification establishes a more direct connection between the theme of the work and the Chapel’s dedication to Santa Maria della Pietà, as well as to its earlier altarpiece, a late sixteenth-century painting by an unknown artist, where Christ is shown lying across the Virgin’s lap in accordance with the Vesperbild type (Forgione 2022, p. 26).

No payment documents relating to the altarpiece have yet come to light; it was executed by Celebrano, who was serving as superintendent of the Chapel as Raimondo’s most trusted collaborator. In 1766, the first edition of the _Breve nota_ attests that work on the relief was in progress at that time (p. 10; cf. Attanasio 2011, p. 59), and it was in all probability completed by the following year. The inventory of assets remaining in the Prince’s estate records that, at that date, the altarpiece was not yet fully ‘polished’ (_allustrata_) (_Inventario_ 1771, fols. 65r–66v; cf. Attanasio 2011, pp. 76, 130), and that a lapis lazuli frame, subsequently executed to Sansevero’s own design, had yet to be added (Sarnelli 1772, p. 130). From the records of the lawsuit that Francesco Maria Queirolo (1704–1762) brought against Raimondo in 1759, it emerges that the sculptor was required to return to his patron even ‘the low-relief wax model of the high altar’ (cf. _Chartulae desangriane_ 2006, p. 81). It cannot be ruled out that this corresponded to one of the ‘thirty-six original models in fired clay’ that Antonio Corradini had made for the decoration of the Chapel and left at his death in 1752 (Origlia Paolino 1754, p. 365).

The decisive role Corradini played in devising the Chapel’s sculptural decoration and his northern cultural background have also, until now, been invoked to explain the choice (unique in Baroque Naples) of adorning the high altar with a marble altarpiece (Cioffi 1994, pp. 24–26). More recently, however, it has been explained, above all, in the light of what the young Raimondo would have seen during his ten years studying with the Jesuits at the Seminario Romano, beginning in 1720 (Forgione 2022, pp. 22–25). In Rome, Di Sangro had occasion to examine the major marble altarpieces of the Baroque period, two of which deserve particular mention: the _Compianto_ by Domenico Guidi (1625–1701) for the high altar of the Cappella del Monte di Pietà, delivered in 1674, whose reputation was advanced by a considerable number of engravings and pictorial adaptations (Giometti 2010, esp. pp. 190–200, no. 25.S); and the _San Luigi Gonzaga in gloria_, which Pierre Le Gros the Younger (1666–1719) made for the Jesuits at Sant’Ignazio in the late seventeenth century, to which Di Sangro and Celebrano also seem to have looked for the _cornice mistilinea_. The Prince’s interest in Roman sculpture is also documented with certainty in the _Libro in folio delle statue esistenti in Roma_ that he lent to Queirolo, evidently for use as a working resource and primary visual reference for the decoration of the Chapel (Forgione 2022, pp. 22–23).

On closer inspection, Celebrano’s altarpiece appears to follow the compositional scheme of the spectacular, naturalistic, and temporary ‘Calvaries’ that, even in the time of the young Raimondo, were erected in Roman churches for the _Quarantore_, an ancient liturgical practice alluding to the brief period Christ spent in the tomb (ibid., pp. 11–18). This iconographic tradition may also shed light on the significance of the sarcophagus being opened by the angel beneath the mensa of the high altar. The episode has until now been linked to the _Resurrection_ (cf., for example, Nappi 2010, p. 106, and Marco Bussagli, in _Napoli, la Cappella Sansevero_ 2019, p. 103). However, unlike in depictions of the _Quem quaeritis_, the angel does not point towards Heaven, nor do we find the narrative context necessary for identifying the subject, with the holy women visiting the tomb and the sleeping guards present. The scene is, therefore, more plausibly to be read as continuous, in space and time, with the _Compianto_ depicted above the altar of the Eucharist. The divine messenger would thus be opening the tomb for the laying out of the corpse. This interpretation would find confirmation in Father Pozzo’s design for the _Quarantore_. The folio, formerly in the collection of art historian Luigi Salerno and later circulating on the Roman art market, depicts several angels bearing Christ to the tomb, while two further angels, set within a majestic, raised ciborium, open the sarcophagus that will soon receive the body of the Saviour. The requirements of sacred theatrical spectacle justify the angel, in Pozzo’s drawing as in Celebrano’s altarpiece, undertaking an action not normally assigned to him in traditional iconography, where it is Joseph of Arimathea who prepares the tomb of the Redeemer together with Nicodemus (Forgione 2022, p. 37).
