**Author of the entry:** Eleonora Loiodice

In the underground _cavea_ of the Sansevero Chapel, a space in which Prince Raimondo had intended to house the _Cristo velato_ (cf. entry 5) and the tombs of his descendants (di Sangro 2018, p. 32), the _Macchine anatomiche_ are now displayed in cases made of glass and wood, and have become an integral part of the Museum’s identity and the interest it attracts. The _Breve nota di quel che si vede in casa del principe di Sansevero don Raimondo di Sangro nella città di Napoli_ (1769, pp. 21–24; cf. Attanasio 2011, pp. 61–62) records that Raimondo di Sangro had originally placed these two figures in the Fenice apartment of his family palace: ‘two anatomical machines are on show, or, more precisely, two skeletons, of a male and a female, in which all the veins and arteries of the human body, made by injection, may be observed’ (_Breve nota_ 1769, p. 22). They are also described in the detailed and astonished account given by Donato Andrea Fantoni (1977, p. 51) during his journey to Naples in 1769: ‘I saw two anatomised machines, of a man and a woman, kept in two separate cabinets’. The two models remained in the apartment at least until 1771, as documented by the inventory of the Prince’s estate compiled in that year (fol. 60r; cf. Attanasio 2011, p. 96). By 1856, however, Camillo Napoleone Sasso (p. 200) records the presence of the two _Macchine_ in the Chapel.

According to a particularly persistent legend, it is said to have been Di Sangro himself who, in the laboratory where he conducted his experiments and studies on palingenesis, injected a substance capable of petrifying and immortalising the arterial and venous systems of two of his servants (Colonna di Stigliano 1895, p. 121; Macci 2006, p. 27). In reality, the two angiographic figures are the work of the Palermitan priest and anatomopathologist Giuseppe Salerno. At the woman’s feet lay the foetus of the child who had died with her (Attanasio 2011, p. 62), described by Fantoni (1977, p. 51) in the palace as ‘well preserved and executed’, despite consisting of ‘minute parts’, ‘liable to break during handling and to deteriorate over time’. The foetus was, however, stolen from the Chapel by unknown persons between 1983 and 1986; by that time, only a few fragments appear to have survived, and it was no longer connected to the adult figure by the umbilical cord.

The first anatomical machine, the male, was exhibited on 5 May 1756 at the Accademia Medica of Palermo, where, ‘in the presence of His Excellency the Viceroy Don Giovanni Fogliani, and before an assembly of learned men, in this our academy our anatomist and physician, Doctor Giuseppe Salerno, priest of Palermo, displayed a skeleton worked with the utmost refinement in every part’ (translation from De Gregorio e Russo 1762, p. 246). The news reached King Charles of Bourbon in Naples, who invited Salerno to give a lecture on his anatomical model to physicians and men of letters. Among those present was the Prince of Sansevero, who, having seen the machine, purchased it for display in his palace and invited Salerno to work for him. Attanasio (2011, p. 95) suggests that the female anatomical machine may also have been produced independently by Salerno and given to the Prince at a later date. This hypothesis derives from the preface to the _Opuscoli di autori siciliani_ (De Gregorio e Russo 1762, p. XVI), which records that Salerno made three additional figures: ‘two of women and one of a man’. It follows that one female machine was supplied to the Prince, identifiable with the exemplar now in the Chapel, while the other pair, described by Giovanni Gorgone (1801–1868), must have remained at the University of Palermo, where all trace of them was lost (Pitrè 1868, p. 53).

Documents preserved in the Archivio Storico del Banco di Napoli (Nappi 2010, pp. 36–37, docs. 68–70) show that the Prince made payments to Salerno between 1763 and 1765, and that the latter, in a contract signed with Sansevero on 1 February 1763, undertook to provide his services for 2,000 ducats, payable in instalments of 150 ducats per year. On 22 April 1765, the carpenter Alessandro Ciulli was also paid seven ducats for supplying a cabinet to house a skeleton (ibid., p. 37, doc. 71). According to the same payment records (ibid., p. 21), Salerno probably worked on a property belonging to the Prince situated opposite the church of Sant’Eframo Nuovo.

Salerno’s work needs to be situated within a broader context. He studied with Giuseppe Mastiani (1715–1756), whose teacher had been Jacques-Bénigne Winslow (1669–1760). Winslow had learned the art of preparing corpses in the Netherlands, where he had met Frederik Ruysch (1638–1731), renowned for his mummies, embalmed using a technique involving injection. More generally, the eighteenth century was the golden age of anatomical wax modelling. The body was to be studied; and, given the difficulty of obtaining corpses, it was reproduced in wax as a complex mechanism to be examined. Well-known centres include Bologna, with Ercole Lelli (1702–1766), Anna Morandi (1714–1774), and Giovanni Manzolini (1700–1774); Florence, with Felice Fontana (1730–1805) and Clemente Michelangelo Susini (1754–1814), remembered for the anatomical _Venuses_ (de Ceglia 2005); Ferrara, with wax models used for obstetric training; Pavia and Milan; and finally the Sicilian tradition, to which Salerno belongs. This tradition began with Giovanni and Jacopo Matinati in the late fifteenth century and continued through the sixteenth, seventeenth, and eighteenth centuries, culminating in figures such as Gaetano Giulio Zumbo (1656–1701), whose wax models are preserved at the Museo di Storia Naturale La Specola of the University of Florence (cf. Gerbino 2013, pp. 6–29, 223–228). A contemporary of Salerno was Paolo Graffeo, whose osteographic models are now lost.

Salerno’s _Macchine anatomiche_ present a complex and delicate network of arteries, veins, and capillaries of varying thickness, colour, and length. In 2007, Lucia Dacome and Renata Peters demonstrated that the circulatory system is composed of iron wire and a particular type of wax. The bones are held together by pins, nails, and metal wires. Both figures include most of the bones of the human body, although many are incorrectly positioned. The skulls have been sawn open, and hinges on either side allow them to be opened, revealing a dense network of blood vessels within. The condition of the uterus suggests that the woman may have died during or shortly after childbirth; and a study co-ordinated in 2013 by Matteo Della Monica established that the models display several cardiovascular anomalies, probably reflecting incomplete anatomical knowledge.

The _Macchine_, significantly placed in the Fenice room, named after the bird associated with the myth of resurrection, evoke the arrested stillness of death. At the time, debate around the question of the end of life sought to determine whether it was ‘a process of disaggregation of the corporeal machine’ (Donato 2014, p. 205), as mechanistic theories proposed, or the ‘loss of a material essence and immaterial principle that is life/soul’, as maintained by vitalists (ibid., p. 207). Di Sangro, an eclectic scholar, addressed the themes of death and resurrection through his inventions and investigations, including those concerning palingenesis, as well as through the works housed in the Chapel. Within this broader and complex debate, he may also have sought to understand, not least through the study of the _Macchine anatomiche_, whether death is a process or an instantaneous event, and whether the human body, when considered as a machine, might be reanimated or brought back to life, phoenix-like.
