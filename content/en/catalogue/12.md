**Author of the entry:** Luigi Coiro

The monument is dedicated to Giulia Gaetani dell’Aquila d’Aragona, consort of Paolo di Sangro, fourth Prince of Sansevero (cf. entry 13). She died in 1636 and is portrayed in three-quarter view in the medallion at the apex of the pyramid that forms the backdrop to the statue of _Liberalità_. The tomb was executed by Francesco Queirolo before the close of 1754 (Origlia 1754, p. 366), a date also recorded in the inscription on the base, probably following one of the thirty-six sculptural models for the Chapel prepared by Antonio Corradini by 1752 (ibid., p. 365).

The ‘animated and ingenious allegory’ (Colapietra 1986a, p. 67) reflects, with some licence, the attributes associated with this virtue in Cesare Ripa’s _Iconologia_. In her right hand, she holds a compass, still visible and intact in an engraving executed shortly before the mid-nineteenth century by Franz Wenzel (pl. XIII). Only a fragment remains, embellished with a delicate ‘_fettuccia_’ (_Inventario_ 1771, fol. 114v; cf. Attanasio 2011, p. 148), and three coins (or medals). The left hand, however, upends a spectacular cornucopia overflowing not with ‘fruits and flowers’, but with ‘jewels, money, necklaces, and other valuable objects’ (Ripa 1765, p. 25), while the eagle – which, moreover, alludes to the family name and coat of arms of the deceased and is described as the ‘most liberal among all birds’ – instead of being placed ‘above the head of the said figure’ (ibid.), is positioned at her feet. The figure is adorned with a banded diadem set with a drop pearl.

It is precisely in these details that the realist vein of the Genoese sculptor (Cioffi 1994, p. 40) emerges once again, as he engages with the various still lifes that enrich the composition without overburdening it: the eagle, certainly, but above all the cornucopia, whose virtuoso and highly polished rendering suits its sumptuous content, which seems on the verge of overflowing beyond the rocky base.

In Marina Picone’s view (1959, p. 76), compared with _Sincerità_ and _Santa Rosalia_ (cf. entries 25 and 7), _Liberalità_ also displays a ‘less stylised language […], but rather a more generous design, richer in decorative vitality’, which softens, ‘in the broken sinuosity of the folds, the typically angular rigidity of the other works’.
