**Author of the entry:** Gianluca Forgione

On 7 November 1748, Raimondo di Sangro paid Francesco Maria Russo one hundred ducats, completing the 150 agreed between artist and patron before the notary Giovanni Bottigliero, for the painting to be executed on the vault of the ‘Pietatella’ (Nappi 2010, pp. 98, 124, doc. 387). The decoration, signed and dated ‘FRANCISCVS RVSSO PINXIT ANNO DOMINI 1749’, is not recorded in the _Breve nota di quel che si vede in casa del principe di Sansevero don Raimondo di Sangro nella città di Napoli_ (1769), apart from a brief reference to the illusionistic dome painted ‘within the great arch of the high altar’ (pp. 12–13; cf. Attanasio 2011, p. 59). The 1771 inventory of the Prince’s estate also treats the work summarily (fols. 127v–128r; cf. Attanasio 2011, pp. 152–153). More revealing is the mention of the _Gloria del Paradiso_ in the will Sansevero drew up in his own hand on 7 August 1770, where the ‘painting of the ceiling’ is listed among the very few works that his first-born, Vincenzo di Sangro, would be permitted to alter, ‘making use of the best painter available’, and with the intention ‘of reworking it in a more refined manner, better conceived and suited to the order and character of the Chapel’s ornaments’ (_Testamento_ 1770, fols. 45v–46r; cf. _Chartulae desangriane_ 2006, p. 107).

Raimondo had in any case already sounded the abilities of the artist to whom he entrusted the first major commission undertaken in the Chapel. As deputy of the Treasury, the Prince had supervised the commission of the _Trionfo della Fede_ frescoed on the vault of the antechamber of the sacristy of the Cappella di San Gennaro in Naples Cathedral, for which ‘Nicolò and Francesco Rossi’ received instalments in January 1744 (Nappi 2010, p. 124, doc. 388; Nicolucci, Conte 2018, p. 104, docs. 1–2). Although the _Gloria del Paradiso_ did not fully satisfy him, Di Sangro nevertheless continued to employ Russo, who, as ‘house painter’, ‘ornamental painter’, and ‘_guazzista_’, repeatedly received payment between March 1763 and March 1765 (Nappi 2010, pp. 125–126, docs. 392–395; Masucci, Spruit, in _Raimondo di Sangro_ 2020, pp. 238, 241, 252, 255). Russo is also credited with the painted architecture in the balustraded tribune to the left of the high altar (Picone 1959, p. 66); the patron’s regard for him is further indicated by his being entrusted with the design of Raimondo’s own mausoleum, whose execution is problematically connected with the date 1759 inscribed on the base (cf. entry 24).

The fresco represents the _Epiphany of Paradise_: a sky filled with light, populated by circling angels and _putti_, centred on the dove of the Holy Spirit bearing the triangular symbol of the Trinity, through which the supernatural world breaks into the centre of the vault, disrupting its illusionistic architecture. Above the arch leading to the high altar, angels support a sun inscribed ‘_Mater Pietatis’_, alluding to the dedication of the Chapel; in the surrounding monochrome medallions, the family saints, Randisio, Berardo (cardinal), Filippa, Rosalia, Oderisio, and Berardo (abbot), witness the beatific vision. In Raimondo’s iconographic vision, the contemplation of Truth is thus the ultimate end of the soul, provided that the deceptive and labyrinthine course of its earthly existence (mirrored in the path of the faithful through the Di Sangro Chapel) has been conducted in accordance with Christian virtues and with faith in the sacrifice of the Son of God, represented in Francesco Celebrano’s _Pietà_ on the high altar (Forgione 2022, pp. 51–54).

The architectural illusionism of the vault, the false dome, and the manifestation of paradisiacal glory recall the most authoritative models of Roman Baroque decoration, which Raimondo had come to know close at hand during his decade of study with the Jesuits at the Seminario Romano from 1720. Russo must have been directed to refer explicitly to the precedents painted in the last quarter of the seventeenth century in the Roman churches of the Gesù and Sant’Ignazio by Giovan Battista Gaulli, known as Baciccio (1639–1709), and Andrea Pozzo (1642–1709). It is very probable that the Prince and his painter studied and discussed Pozzo’s designs through the engraved plates of the Jesuit’s well-known treatise on perspective, published in two volumes between 1693 and 1700 (Cioffi 1994, pp. 65–68). To realise a project of this derivative kind, Raimondo appears to have selected a painter, formed within a Solimenesque milieu, who had also acquired practical experience in _quadratura_, plausibly in connection with theatrical scenography (Nicolucci, Conte 2018). The evident disparity in quality between the Roman models and Russo’s results in the illusionistic and anamorphic treatment of space, together with a chromatic intensity perhaps not entirely in keeping with the overall tone of the Chapel (de Sangro 1991, p. 147), led Raimondo, as noted, to include the vault painting among the few elements of the Chapel that his heir was authorised to rework.
